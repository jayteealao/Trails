# Notes from a Slow Garden — Field Journal

*Ada Brennan*

Published: 2026-03-14T08:30:00Z

The garden behind the café was never meant to be a project. It began
          with a single pot of thyme on the step, and a promise that nothing
          would be bought that could be grown, swapped or found.
        
        
          By the second spring the step had become a terrace of crates, and the
          crates had become a habit. Every morning before opening, the owner
          watered them in the same order: thyme, sage, the rosemary that refused
          to die, then the beans that climbed the drainpipe.
        
        
          What changed first was not the plants but the people. Regulars began
          to bring cuttings & seeds wrapped in napkins. A retired teacher left
          a note that read “tomatoes want more sun than you think”, and
          she was right. Nobody asked for anything in return.
        
        
          There is a temptation, in any slow thing, to measure it. The owner kept
          a notebook for a while — dates, heights, the first flower — and
          then stopped. It wasn’t that the numbers were wrong; it was that
          they missed the point. The garden was a reason to stand still for ten
          minutes, and the notebook made those minutes into work.
        
        
          A few things did turn out to matter, and they are worth writing down
          for anyone who wants to start:
        
        
          Water in the morning, never at noon.
          Keep one crate empty, for whatever someone brings next.
          Label nothing; ask instead.
        
        
          The watering schedule, such as it is, lives on a card taped inside the
          door. It has not changed in three years:
        
        Mon  thyme, sage
Wed  rosemary, beans
Fri  everything, twice
        
          Last autumn a neighbour photographed the terrace for a local
          newsletter, and the post brought a dozen visitors in a week. Most came
          once. Two came back with seedlings. One of them, a student named
          Renée, now runs the swap table on Saturdays from nine until the
          coffee runs out.
        
        
          If there is a lesson, it is an old one. A place becomes shared when
          people are allowed to leave something in it. You can read more about
          the swap table in the notes on the swap
          table, or simply come by on a Saturday and see it for yourself.
